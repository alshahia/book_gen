## Presentation

### Chapter length and rhythm

The book is **5 chapters × ~25 pages** (~125 pages total). This is the smallest defensible nonfiction book for the topic and the rhythm drives the structural conventions below.

- **~25 pages per chapter** is intentional. Long enough to install one concrete move with its evidence and tradeoffs; short enough that the reader can read a chapter in one sitting and execute the move the same day. A chapter that lands under ~15 pages is doing less than the outline asks of it; one that runs over ~35 pages is probably trying to install two moves.
- **Chapter 1 is independent.** It can be read and executed first, but it does not require any prior chapter to be in place. Its move (the morning routine / structured start) is the foundation every later chapter builds on.
- **Chapters 2–5 form a linear chain.** Each later chapter's concrete move depends on the prior chapter's installed element — ch-02 protects the morning block that ch-01 plans; ch-03 drains the system ch-01 fills and captures the next steps ch-02 produces; ch-04 scales ch-03's mini weekly review to the full weekly cadence while protecting ch-01 and ch-02 at weekly scale; ch-05 repairs whichever installed element has broken. The chain is what allows the book to spend ~25 pages per chapter rather than re-justifying the whole system in each one.
- **Internal rhythm.** Each chapter alternates between explanation and concrete moves at roughly 2:1 prose-to-instruction. No chapter is a pure recitation of evidence, and no chapter is a pure checklist. The closing 10–15% of every chapter is dedicated to the reader-facing action (see "Outcome lines" below).

### Structural devices

**Subheadings.** Frequent. A chapter should be scannable in 30 seconds. Subheadings carry the navigational load; the reader should always be able to answer "what is this paragraph about?" by reading the subheading two lines up. Subheadings are sentence-fragment style, not full sentences, and they describe the *move* the section installs, not the topic being discussed ("Block the morning, not the day" not "Deep work").

**Lists.** Sparingly. Lists are for items the reader is expected to *do* in sequence or to enumerate as a discrete set. The four-step shutdown ritual (ch-03), the renewal-four dimensions (ch-04), and the two-missed-days rule (ch-05) are appropriate list material. Bulleted prose — paragraphs that have been converted into bullets because the author lost the patience to write them — is not.

**Callouts.** Yes, but only one kind per chapter, and used at most twice per chapter. Two recurring callout types:

1. **"The move"** — a single boxed sentence stating the chapter's concrete action in imperative form. Appears once, near the end of the chapter, immediately before the closing.
2. **"Evidence vs. convention"** — a short boxed distinction used when the chapter is presenting a number that is widely cited but not directly supported by the cited evidence (e.g., the 90–120 minute block in ch-02, per decision-001). This callout names the convention, names the underlying evidence, and tells the reader the evidence is thinner than the popularity suggests. See Vocabulary constraints for the exact phrasing required.

No other callout types. No "pro tip" boxes. No "war story" boxes. No "common mistakes" boxes. If the writer wants to break the rule, escalate to master first.

**Chapter-opening convention.** Every chapter opens with a concrete scene — a clock time, a specific moment, a small physical detail — that anchors the *problem* the chapter solves in the reader's day. Not a thesis statement. Not a chapter summary. A scene. The scene should make the reader see themselves in the problem before the chapter names the move.

**Chapter-closing convention.** Every chapter closes with **one concrete action the reader takes the same day they finish reading**. The action is the chapter's outcome line from the outline, surfaced verbatim or near-verbatim as the final imperative line. No "in this chapter we explored..." closings. No "tomorrow, try this..." closings that defer the action. The reader closes the book, walks to their calendar, and does the thing.

**Reading aids.** A short end-of-chapter "What's next" note (one or two sentences) is permitted in chapters 2–5 to bridge to the next chapter's dependency. Chapter 1 has no such note (it is independent). Chapter 5 has no "What's next" (it closes the book).

### Outcome lines → reader-facing actions

The outline gives each chapter an outcome line. The style guide treats those outcome lines as the binding contract for what the chapter's closing action must be. The writer may not substitute a "richer" action, a "more interesting" action, or a "deeper" action in place of the outcome line. The action is the point.

| Chapter | Outcome line (from outline) | Reader-facing action the closing must deliver |
|---|---|---|
| ch-01 | Reader writes tomorrow's first task and its cue on a card (or in their task tool) before doing anything else tomorrow morning. | The closing instructs the reader to write tonight, before sleep, the single first task for tomorrow and the cue that will trigger it. Imperative. Specific. |
| ch-02 | Reader blocks out one 60–120 minute window in tomorrow's morning, names the task, identifies one reactive source to exclude. | The closing instructs the reader to open their calendar *now*, block the morning slot, name the task, and pick the one reactive source to keep out. Imperative. |
| ch-03 | Reader runs the four-step shutdown ritual today and says the cue phrase before leaving the workspace. | The closing instructs the reader to run the four steps this evening and say the cue phrase on the way out. Imperative. With the four steps named in order. |
| ch-04 | Reader schedules the next weekly review on the calendar and attaches it to an existing cue (typically the Friday shutdown). | The closing instructs the reader to schedule the next review now and attach it to the existing Friday-shutdown cue. Imperative. |
| ch-05 | Reader can answer "which single cue will I restore this week?" and has the two-missed-days rule written down as the trigger. | The closing instructs the reader to write down the two-missed-days rule, pick one cue to restore, and commit to it for three consecutive days. Imperative. |

The chapter does not need to *prove* the reader did the action — the book is not a quiz. But the closing must be specific enough that the reader could not confuse it with general advice.

## Voice

The book addresses a working knowledge worker who already uses a calendar and a task tool, has heard of deep work and time blocking, and is skeptical of productivity-as-identity content. The voice is built by naming the specific traits to emulate from the three reference points in `intake.md` — not by saying "write like Newport" or "write like Clear" wholesale.

### Specific traits to emulate

**From Cal Newport (Deep Work, Slow Productivity):**

- **Calm, evidence-cited posture.** Every empirical claim has a named source. Not "studies show" — "Lally's 2010 study found a 66-day median." Not "experts recommend" — "Newport argues..." (with the book title). When the evidence is thinner than the popular framing suggests, the voice says so out loud (per decision-001, ch-02). When the evidence is contested, the voice presents the contested status, not a confident side.
- **Anti-hustle posture.** No motivational slogans. No "you can do it." No "this will change your life." The book is allowed to be confident; it is not allowed to be hype. The reader is a peer, not a prospect.
- **Specific commitments rather than aspirational slogans.** "Block 90 minutes, name the task, exclude one reactive source" is acceptable. "Make time for focus" is not. The book names what the reader does, not the feeling the reader will have.

**From The Pragmatic Programmer (Hunt & Thomas):**

- **Direct address to the reader.** Second person. "You block 90 minutes." Not "one blocks 90 minutes" or "the reader is advised to block 90 minutes." Direct address is the dominant mode; passive voice is a flag for prose that has lost its grip.
- **No filler.** Every sentence in the chapter must do work: install a move, cite evidence, surface a tradeoff, or hand off to the next paragraph. Sentences that exist to soften the previous sentence are filler. Sentences that exist to set up the next sentence are filler *unless* the setup is the actual content.
- **Every chapter ends with a concrete action.** This is the most distinctive trait to adopt and the most consequential. The closing imperative is the chapter's whole point. (See Presentation: "Outcome lines → reader-facing actions.")

**From Atomic Habits (Clear) — with the ch-05 constraint from decision-003 applied:**

- **Concrete habit-installation language.** The book uses Clear's *vocabulary* of cues, routines, and rewards where it is the most accurate word for the thing — but not the four-laws causal model ("make it obvious / attractive / easy / satisfying") as the primary frame. In ch-04, Clear's *habit-stacking syntax* (the "after [current habit], I will [new habit]" formula) is acceptable as a structural device. In ch-05, Clear's four-laws vocabulary is forbidden as the primary frame; the chapter must cite Lally (66-day median, 18–254 day range) and Wood (habit persistence via environmental cues) directly, and Clear may be mentioned only as a popularizer whose "never miss twice" rule is consistent with the underlying evidence. This is the constraint from decision-003 and is binding.
- **Why Clear is partial, not the whole model.** Clear's four-laws recipe is treated by the book as a popular and useful mnemonic, not as the empirical foundation. The empirical foundation is the underlying habit-formation literature (Lally 2010, Wood 2016), and the book cites that literature directly when the chapter is making a claim, not Clear.

### Formality level

**Conversational professional.** Like a senior engineer explaining a system to a peer — not a coach hyping a transformation, not an academic hedging a position. The reader is a working professional and the book talks to them as one.

- Contractions yes ("you've," "don't," "isn't"). Feels human, not lecture-y.
- No exclamation marks. The book is not enthusiastic. The book is confident.
- No second-person cheerleading ("you've got this," "trust the process"). The reader is a peer, not a client.
- No academic hedging clichés ("it could be argued that," "some scholars suggest"). Either cite the scholar by name and the year, or don't cite.
- No productivity jargon from outside the three reference points unless it earns its place. Avoid: "synergy," "leverage" (as a verb), "optimize" (used generally), "deep dive," "unpack." Permit: "cue," "ritual," "shutdown," "block," "weekly review," "deep work" (as Newport's term, scoped), "deliberate practice" (as Ericsson's term, scoped).

### Person

**Second person, dominant.** Direct address to the reader as the practitioner of the system. "You block 90 minutes" — not "the practitioner blocks 90 minutes" or "we block 90 minutes." First-person plural ("we") is rare and reserved for one use: describing the *shared problem* the reader and the book are looking at together ("we both know that the morning starts reactive"). Even there, sparingly, and never as a substitute for you.

The book is not narrated in first person. The author is not a character. The author is a careful guide.

### Pacing and rhythm

- **Short sentences for key claims and concrete moves.** "Block 90 minutes. Name the task. Exclude one reactive source." These are the load-bearing sentences of the book and they should read as load-bearing.
- **Longer sentences for explanation and evidence.** When the chapter is laying out a study, naming a tradeoff, or qualifying a claim, the sentence length opens up. The book is not a staccato of four-word declaratives.
- **Mix of rhythm, not monotone.** A paragraph of short moves followed by a paragraph of evidence followed by a paragraph of one mixed-length sentence doing the transition is the goal. The reader should feel the chapter breathing, not marching.
- **One paragraph per move, then its evidence-nut.** The default rhythm is: state the move in one or two sentences, then give the evidence or the trade-off in the next paragraph or two. Do not bundle two moves in one paragraph. Do not split one move across three paragraphs.

### Vocabulary constraints driven by resolved decisions

These are binding. The writer must not violate them; the reviewer will fail chapters that do.

**From decision-001 (ch-02):** The 90–120 minute block is the chapter's recommended length, but the body must explicitly flag the uncertainty. Required elements in the ch-02 body:

- The chapter recommends 90–120 minutes as the working block.
- The chapter must name the source of the 90–120 minute figure (the ultradian cycle research, Hobson & Pace-Schott 2002) and note that it originates from sleep-stage research, not waking attention.
- The chapter must contrast the 90–120 minute figure with Ericsson's 60–90 minute finding from elite deliberate-practice data, and note that Ericsson does not specifically study knowledge workers at desks.
- The chapter must name Csikszentmihalyi as the source of flow theory and note that flow theory does not give a specific block length.
- Permitted phrasing patterns: "the conventional 90–120 minute block," "the evidence is thinner than it looks," "the underlying research supports the principle (single-task, low-interruption) more than the specific number."
- Forbidden: presenting the 90–120 minute block as an empirical finding, as optimal, or as "research-proven" without the qualification.

**From decision-002 (ch-01 and ch-03, justification only):** The morning routine's duration and the shutdown ritual's value must not be justified on willpower-depletion grounds.

- In ch-01: the 30-minute-or-less ceiling is offered as a convention the reader can adjust, not as an evidence-backed optimum. The framing is "structured start" — the value is the cognitive scaffolding, not the duration.
- In ch-03: the shutdown ritual's value rests on cognitive offloading and working-memory management, not on ego depletion. The chapter must not present the shutdown as "restoring willpower" or "recovering from decision fatigue."
- Forbidden vocabulary across ch-01 and ch-03: "ego depletion," "willpower depletion," "decision fatigue," "depletion of self-control" as causal explanations for the duration or the ritual. These may be *named* as a popular but contested framing if the chapter is explicitly noting the popular framing's contested status, but they may not be presented as the chapter's justification.
- Required vocabulary: "structured start," "cognitive offloading," "cognitive scaffolding," "working-memory management," "environmental cue," "stable trigger."

**From decision-003 (ch-05):** Clear's four-laws vocabulary is not the primary frame; Lally/Wood evidence is cited directly.

- The chapter's primary causal model is the habit-formation literature (Lally: 66-day median, 18–254 day range; Wood: habit persistence via environmental cues).
- Clear may be mentioned once, parenthetically, as a popularizer whose "never miss twice" rule is consistent with the underlying evidence. Required phrasing pattern: "Clear's popular 'never miss twice' rule is consistent with Lally's finding that [X] and Wood's finding that [Y]."
- Forbidden in ch-05: "make it obvious," "make it attractive," "make it easy," "make it satisfying" as the chapter's primary framework. The four-laws vocabulary is not the chapter's spine.
- The "never miss twice" rule is permitted as a closing aphorism *only if* preceded by the Lally/Wood citation that it is consistent with.

**Vocabulary blacklist (all chapters):** The writer must not use the following without explicit qualification:

- "Decision fatigue" — unless used to name the popular framing as contested.
- "Ego depletion" / "willpower depletion" — as causal justification.
- "Optimal" — without an evidence-backed citation.
- "Proven" / "scientifically proven" — without a citation to a named study.
- "Studies show" — without naming the study.
- "We all know" / "everyone knows" — the reader is a peer; the book does not assume shared intuition.
- "Make it obvious / attractive / easy / satisfying" as a primary frame in ch-05.

**Vocabulary allowed without restriction:**

- "Convention," "heuristic," "rule of thumb," "rule" — when naming a practice that is useful but not empirically optimized.
- "Evidence suggests," "the data show," "Lally's 2010 study found," "Newport's argument in *Deep Work* is..." — when citing.
- "Cue," "routine," "reward," "environment," "trigger" — habit-vocabulary, used precisely.
- "Block," "ritual," "shutdown," "review," "system" — the book's own vocabulary for what it installs.
