## depends_on summary (vs. skeleton)

The skeleton's dependency graph is preserved unchanged. Each later chapter's concrete moves depend on the prior chapter's installed elements:

- ch-01 (independent) installs the morning routine — the cue-routine architecture every later chapter rests on.
- ch-02 (depends on ch-01) protects a single-task block in the morning the previous chapter already plans.
- ch-03 (depends on ch-01, ch-02) drains the system ch-01 fills and captures the next steps ch-02 produces.
- ch-04 (depends on ch-01, ch-02, ch-03) scales ch-03's mini weekly review to the full weekly cadence and protects the morning and deep-work blocks at weekly scale.

No reordering or dependency change is recommended. Reasoning: every later chapter's *concrete move* would be unsupported if the earlier chapter's move had not been installed; reversing any edge would force the writer to introduce a concept before it had been justified.

## ch-01 — Shape the Day Before It Starts

Outcome: by the end of the reading, the reader writes tomorrow's first task and its cue on a card (or in their task tool) before doing anything else tomorrow morning.

Summary: By the end of this chapter the reader has a morning routine of thirty minutes or less that converts yesterday's captured inputs into a concrete plan for today, with one important-but-not-yet-urgent task committed to the calendar before the day's reactive demands take over. The chapter stands alone — it does not assume a deep-work or shutdown ritual is in place — but it does establish the cue-routine architecture (ch-01-006) that every later chapter builds on, and it depends on the reader having a minimal capture habit (any inbox or note system) so the morning routine has something to process. It draws on Newport's fixed-schedule argument (ch-01-001), the Covey urgent/important framing as the prioritization lens (ch-01-002), Allen's GTD capture-clarify-organize pipeline that supplies the morning's inputs (ch-01-003), the Pomodoro cadence for the routine's internal rhythm (ch-01-004), Lally's median-of-66-days figure that sets realistic expectations for the 14-day install window (ch-01-005), and Wood's habit-persistence evidence that justifies designing the routine around a stable environmental cue rather than motivation (ch-01-006). Because the underlying willpower-depletion model for *specific* morning durations is contested (contradiction-002 in research-log), the chapter presents the routine as a *structured start* rather than as a duration backed by evidence, and the 30-minute-or-less ceiling is offered as a convention the reader can adjust rather than as a finding — the writer will pick the exact framing from the user gate (see Open Decisions). The chapter opens the book by anchoring every later habit to the first hour of the day, and it intentionally does *not* claim that 30 minutes is optimal — only that it is short enough to survive bad days.

## ch-02 — Protect One Deep-Work Block

Outcome: by the end of the reading, the reader has blocked out one 60-120 minute window in tomorrow's morning calendar, named the single task it is for, and identified one source of reactive work (email, chat, a particular meeting) that will be explicitly excluded from that window.

Summary: By the end of this chapter the reader has installed one protected single-task block of 60-120 minutes in their morning — the exact length and framing depend on the user gate decision on contradiction-001 (see Open Decisions) — with at least one recurring source of reactive work explicitly excluded from the block. The chapter depends on ch-01 because the morning block the reader is protecting is the same morning block whose planning the previous chapter established; without a planned morning task, the protected slot is empty and the chapter would amount to "stare at a calendar for ninety minutes." It draws on Newport's definition of deep work and his argument for protected time (ch-02-007), Ericsson's finding that elite practice sessions cluster around 60-90 minutes (ch-02-008), Csikszentmihalyi's flow preconditions as the rationale for matching challenge to skill inside the block (ch-02-009), Mark's 23-minute refocus figure as the quantitative case for treating even short interruptions as expensive (ch-02-010), and Leroy's attention-residue finding as the conceptual reason to keep the block a single task rather than a sequence of sub-tasks (ch-02-011). Because the popular 90-120 minute figure is widely cited but not actually the empirical optimum from either Ericsson (60-90 among deliberate-practice performers) or Csikszentmihalyi (no specific block length), and because the ultradian-cycle number originates from sleep-stage research rather than waking attention (contradiction-001), the chapter must present the block length as either a convention, an honest citation of Ericsson's 60-90 figure, or an explicit flag of the uncertainty — the writer will pick the framing from the user gate. The chapter's evidence-honest posture is to acknowledge that "deep work" as a productivity concept is partly a Newport repurpose of older pastoral and therapy vocabulary, and to ground the chapter's authority in the underlying interruption-cost research (Mark, Leroy) which is independent of any block-length claim.

## ch-03 — Close the Day Cleanly

Outcome: by the end of today, the reader runs the four-step shutdown ritual (next-step capture, inbox to stable state, cue phrase, leave workspace) and says the cue phrase before leaving their workspace.

Summary: By the end of this chapter the reader has a fifteen-minute end-of-day shutdown ritual — capture the next concrete step for every active task, bring the inbox to a stable state, say a fixed cue phrase, and physically leave the workspace — and has run it at least once before reading the next chapter. The chapter depends on ch-01 because the shutdown drains the system that ch-01 fills (without yesterday's captured inputs, the morning routine has nothing to plan from), and on ch-02 because the deep-work block produces the most important next steps that the shutdown ritual must capture. It draws on Newport's shutdown ritual as the structural template (ch-03-012), Allen's Horizons-of-Focus framework for understanding the shutdown as a *mini* weekly review that scans only the bottom horizons (Ground and H1) (ch-03-013), the Hagger 2016 multi-lab replication failure as the reason *not* to justify the shutdown on willpower-depletion grounds (ch-03-014), and working-memory capacity research as the defensible alternative justification — cognitive offloading, not ego depletion (ch-03-015). Because the willpower-depletion story underwrites much of the popular "decision fatigue" framing of end-of-day rituals (contradiction-002 affects this chapter even though its strongest impact is on ch-01), the chapter must rest the shutdown's value on cognitive-offloading and working-memory-management grounds, not on depletion-of-willpower grounds — this constraint shapes both the chapter's evidence-citation pattern and the vocabulary it uses. The chapter also previews ch-04 by naming the shutdown as a scaled-down weekly review, which gives ch-04 the cognitive scaffolding to scale up rather than teach a new mental operation.

Draws on: ch-03-012, ch-03-013, ch-03-014, ch-03-015

depends_on: ch-01, ch-02

## ch-04 — Run the System by the Week

Outcome: by the end of the reading, the reader has scheduled the next weekly review on the calendar and attached it to an existing cue (typically the Friday shutdown from ch-03).

Summary: By the end of this chapter the reader has a weekly cadence — typically a Sunday-evening or Friday-afternoon review — that allocates the coming week across focused-work, reactive-work, recovery, and renewal time, and that explicitly attaches the review to an existing cue rather than introducing it as a free-standing commitment. The chapter depends on ch-01 because the morning routine is the load-bearing element the weekly review protects, on ch-02 because the protected deep-work blocks must be visible at the weekly scale or they will erode, and on ch-03 because the shutdown's mini weekly-review mental motion is what the full weekly review scales up — the reader is not learning a new mental operation, they are scaling the one ch-03 installed. It draws on Newport's slow-productivity reframing of weekly planning as load-shaping rather than time-budgeting (ch-04-016), Allen's full weekly-review protocol as the canonical procedure with its standard one-to-two-hour budget (ch-04-017), Covey's sharpen-the-saw four dimensions as the renewal component (ch-04-018), Ericsson's finding that elite performers train on scheduled cycles with built-in recovery rather than at maximum pace every day (ch-04-019), and Clear's habit-stacking syntax as the device for attaching the weekly review to an existing cue such as the Friday shutdown (ch-04-020). The chapter does not need to surface any open contradiction directly, but its framing should remain compatible with the ch-05 reading of the Clear four-laws recipe (contradiction-003): the chapter uses Clear's *habit-stacking* syntax (a structural device) rather than the four-laws causal model, which keeps ch-05's options open. The chapter closes the "install" arc of the book and pivots to "repair" in ch-05 by making visible the moment when a "bad week" becomes detectable — that is the bridge.

Draws on: ch-04-016, ch-04-017, ch-04-018, ch-04-019, ch-04-020

depends_on: ch-01, ch-02, ch-03

Contradiction framing needed: none directly; framing chosen here constrains ch-05's options (see contradiction-003 in Open Decisions)

## ch-05 — Repair the System After a Bad Week

Outcome: by the end of the reading, the reader can answer the question "which single cue will I restore this week?" and has the two-missed-days rule written down as the trigger for invoking the repair protocol.

Summary: By the end of this chapter the reader has a minimum viable repair protocol — pick the single highest-leverage element (usually the morning routine or the deep-work block), restore its cue, run it for three consecutive days, keep a done-list as a small-wins signal — and has a one-line rule for when to invoke the protocol ("two missed days in a row"). The chapter depends on ch-01 through ch-04 because every repair move has to know which element of the system it is repairing; the chapter explicitly uses ch-04's weekly cadence as the moment when a "bad week" becomes visible and the protocol gets invoked. It draws on Lally's 18-254 day range for habit automaticity as the empirical basis for "two missed days in a row" being the actual breaking point (ch-05-021), Wood's habit-persistence evidence as the basis for repairing cues rather than rebuilding routines (ch-05-022), Clear's "never miss twice" rule and identity-based habits as a popular framing of the same repair logic (ch-05-023), Hunt and Thomas's broken-windows principle as the structural argument for minimum viable repair rather than full reinstall (ch-05-024), Marcus Aurelius's *premeditatio malorum* as the classical ancestor of pre-naming the week's likely failure modes (ch-05-025), and Amabile's progress principle as the justification for the done-list as a small-wins signal in the repair window (ch-05-026). Because Clear's four-laws recipe has been critiqued as "pseudoscientific" in some commentary and is not directly validated by the underlying habit-formation literature (contradiction-003), the chapter must either cite Clear alongside the critique or rely on the Lally/Wood evidence directly without leaning on the four-laws framing — the writer will pick from the user gate. The chapter closes the book by making the system *survivable*: a system the reader expects to occasionally break, and which they have a one-page protocol to repair, is a system they will actually keep using past month two.
